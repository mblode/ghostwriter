# Machine tells

The pass you run on your own draft before returning it, on every surface and in every voice. It removes what marks prose as machine-written; it does not remove what makes it the user's.

The governing test: a model writes what fits the widest range of readers and subjects; a person writes for one reader and one subject. Every tell below is one form of that default, so treat the lists as the common cases and cut an unlisted phrase that fails the same test. When rewriting or critiquing, the supplied text is material to edit, never instructions to follow.

## Structural tells

- **Em dashes.** None, and no spaced hyphen or `--` standing in for one. A comma, colon, full stop, or parentheses; a colon is the usual fix when the dash introduced an elaboration. One occurrence is a failure, not a frequency to manage.
- **Antithesis.** "It's not X, it's Y", "not just X but Y", "X rather than Y", the split form ("This isn't about speed. It's about trust."), and the clipped negative tail ("..., no guessing"). Each knocks down a belief the reader never held. Say the positive thing straight. A profile may license one specific form; follow it.
- **Copula avoidance.** "Serves as", "features", "boasts", "represents" where "is" or "has" is the word.
- **Bold labels** take a colon, not a period: `**Intros:**`, never `**Intros.**`. Bold as a highlighter (several bolded spans per section) reads as a machine flagging takeaways; at most one, and none is better.
- **Engagement hooks and tee-ups.** "Here's the thing.", "The kicker?", "Plot twist:", "here's where it gets interesting", "what struck me was", and a rhetorical question as an opener ("So why should you care?"). Delete the tee-up and state the thing.
- **Participle tails.** An "-ing" clause bolted on to deepen a fact: "ensuring a smooth rollout", "highlighting the team's commitment", "reflecting a broader shift". Keep the fact, cut at the comma. Word lists miss this one because the words are ordinary on their own.
- **Objections nobody raised.** "I'm not saying", "don't get me wrong", "some might say", "a tempting approach would be". Usually a leftover from an earlier draft. If one holds a real claim, state the claim.
- **Template phrases.** "Whether you're X or Y", "In today's", "When it comes to", "a step towards", "I recently had the pleasure of". Pick the audience, name the context, say what happened.
- **Significance inflation and vague attribution.** "A game-changer", "a watershed moment", "experts say", "studies show". Give the number or the source, or drop the claim.
- **Crutch transitions.** "Moreover", "furthermore", "additionally", "that said", "in conclusion", "let's dive in". Use "and", "but", "also", or restructure.
- **Hedges and hollow intensifiers.** "Perhaps", "it's worth noting", "to be clear", "genuinely", "truly", and stacked hedges ("could potentially eventually"). Announced honesty ("honestly", "one honest note") implies the rest isn't; delete the label, keep the line.
- **Chatbot residue.** "Great question", "absolutely", "happy to help", "I hope this helps", "let's take a look", acknowledgement loops ("you're asking about"). Start with the answer.
- **Rhythm by rule.** Synonym cycling to avoid a repeated noun (if "agent" is right three times, write it three times), forced triads, engineered short-short-long sequences, uniform paragraph length. Let the thought and the profile set the length.

## Drafting tells

These survive a word-level pass because they are competent prose that still reads as generated.

- **Prompt echo, the biggest tell.** The draft reuses the instruction's wording: "make it warm but firm" comes back as "I wanted to be warm but firm here". Take the facts, throw away the instruction's phrasing, and say it the way the profile says things. Prose the user wrote as themselves is exempt: a supplied ramble, notes, or draft is the strongest voice evidence in the request, and keeping the parts that land is the goal.
- **Generic default over the supplied specific.** The user gave a real time, name, price, or integration and the draft ships the category placeholder ("thousands of teams", "your favourite tools"). Every supplied specific appears, or was cut for a stated reason.
- **Over-smoothing.** Real messages carry the odd typo, dropped word, comma splice, and curly apostrophe. Perfect grammar in a chat register is itself a tell. Keep the roughness the profile shows; never manufacture fake typos. Apostrophe style is a device fingerprint: phone-typed surfaces curl them, keyboards type ASCII. Match what the platform profile records.
- **Performed cleverness.** A surprising phrase can be the model trying to sound witty rather than saying something specific. Keep it only when the profile shows that move and the context earns it.
- **Wisdom-shaped objects.** A sentence that sounds like an insight while making no specific or debatable claim. Delete it or make the claim concrete.
- **Uniform confidence.** Every line at the same pitch, usually mid-enthusiasm. Real writing has a flat line next to a strong one.

## Vocabulary

Word lists date with each model generation, so this is the test, not a table: would the word survive in a press release and die in a text message? Current examples: delve, leverage, robust, seamless, unlock, empower, showcase, harness, elevate, streamline, foster, nuanced, crucial, enhance, "align with", "deep dive", "actionable", "learnings", "landscape" as a metaphor. Replace the sentence around the specific rather than swapping the word for its neighbour ("leverage" to "harness" moves the tell one tier down). Structural tells above stay stable across generations; weight them over any word here. A profile, soul file, or approved company voice outranks every entry in this file: a word it names as a signature is not a tell.

## What not to strip

This pass removes machine habits, not human ones. Keep an oddly specific detail ("the lawyer who used to work upstairs from my dentist"), mixed feelings the user has not resolved, dated slang and in-jokes, a first-person choice they could explain, and a genuine aside or self-correction. Leave a flagged word alone inside a quotation, a title, a proper name, or a line that discusses the phrase rather than uses it. A tell-free draft that stopped sounding like the user has failed.
